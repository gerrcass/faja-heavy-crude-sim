Orinoco Belt · Venezuela · Field guide 01

The oil is there.
The problem is moving it.

Extra-heavy crude in the Faja Petrolífera del Orinoco is roughly 8–10° API and thousands of centipoise at reservoir conditions. It is produced in situ, lifted through sand, heated or diluted, and finally upgraded. This guide follows one barrel through that chain.

A field manual, not a forecast. Every public number is sourced; every generated number is labelled illustrative.

01 The deposit

A foreland basin filled with sand

The Faja sits in the East Venezuela Basin, a foreland basin south of the Caribbean–South America fold belt. Oil generated in the deeper basin migrated updip into shallow Miocene sandstones of the Oficina Formation (USGS 2009). Those sands are fluvial, nearshore-marine and tidal: porous, permeable and largely unconsolidated, but cut by shale barriers and facies changes that make every average a poor description of the next well (SPE 69697).

The oil is biodegraded. Bacteria removed the light ends, leaving a dense, viscous, asphaltic crude with sulfur, vanadium and nickel. One upgrader feed carried about 4.3 wt% sulfur and 3.2 mg KOH/g total acid number (NACE 2006). That is not a nuisance at the edges of the process. It is the reason the surface train has a diluent loop and an upgrader at all.

API gravity is an inverse density scale: a lower number means a denser oil. At 8–10° API the crude is close to, or slightly denser than, water. Viscosity is the other half of the problem: 2,000–8,000 cP at reservoir conditions, against about 1 cP for water. The resource is not the constraint. Mobility is.

Not mined

The Faja is an in-situ resource. There is no oil-sands mining here: no shovels, no trucks, no tailings ponds. Production means wells, pumps, heat and surface process.

Three quantities that are not the same

Original oil in place is the oil estimated to be in the rock before production. Technically recoverable is the fraction that existing or foreseeable technology could extract, regardless of price. Production is a rate: barrels per day from wells that have actually been drilled and connected. A large OOIP says nothing about how fast the field can produce, and a large technically recoverable number says nothing about whether the capital, rigs, steam plants and upgrader capacity exist to produce it.

Cold production recovers a single-digit-to-low-teens percentage of OOIP. Thermal methods can reach several times that. The gap between those two numbers is the reason the next section exists.

02 Cold start

Reach the sand sideways

A vertical well in a thin, heterogeneous sand sees a few metres of pay. A horizontal well turns inside the reservoir and runs through it, exposing hundreds to thousands of metres of sand to one wellbore. Faja developments are built from pads: one surface location, many directional and horizontal wells, one gathering system.

Petrozuata drilled 101 single-lateral and 140 multilateral wells in Zuata between 1998 and 2001 (SPE 78969). The laterals were typically on the order of 1–2 km. A multilateral well adds branches from one main bore, increasing reservoir contact without adding a surface location.

The sand is the second problem. Oficina sands are unconsolidated; they move with the oil. Operators either produce sand deliberately or hold it back with slotted liners, screens or gravel packs. Sand movement can open high-permeability channels, called wormholes, and support a foamy-oil flow regime in which dissolved gas comes out of solution as tiny bubbles and helps drive viscous oil toward the well (SPE 79025).

The same sand erodes pumps, fills separators and settles in tanks. Sand management is not a cleanup detail at the end of the process; it is a design condition that follows the fluid from the completion to the knock-out drum (SPE 184968).

Cold production is cheap relative to steam, and it is the baseline development method. It is also limited: 6–9% recovery in the Faja is a common range. That is enough to justify the wells, but not enough to drain the resource. Thermal EOR is the next step, not an optional extra.

03 Heat

Steam is a loan against viscosity

Viscosity falls steeply as temperature rises. That is the whole thermal proposition: heat the oil and it moves. The cost is that making steam consumes fuel gas and treated water, and the heat is uneven. Cyclic steam stimulation (CSS, “huff and puff”) is the common starting point: inject steam into a well for weeks, shut it in for days to weeks, then produce the heated oil from the same well for weeks to months. Steam is typically 300–340 °C (SPE 188651).

Steam flooding uses separate injectors and producers. HASD — horizontal alternating steam drive — was studied specifically for the Faja to improve sweep in thin, heterogeneous sands (SPE 117689). It is not SAGD. SAGD needs thick, continuous pay and a steam chamber that can grow by gravity drainage; Faja reservoirs are often thinner and more heterogeneous. SAGD pilots exist, but SAGD is not the default story here.

Steam quality is the mass fraction of steam that is vapour in a steam–water mixture. Conventional field generators deliver roughly 70–80% quality; high-quality pilots have tested 95–99% (SPE 218657). Higher quality means more latent heat per tonne of water, so the same oil production needs less water and less fuel.

The system-level efficiency number is the steam-oil ratio: barrels of steam, measured as cold-water equivalent, per barrel of oil produced. CSS projects commonly run in the 3–5 range; poor projects run higher. SOR drives fuel gas, water treatment and CO₂. It is the number to watch when someone claims a thermal process is efficient.

Water is the loop inside the loop. Steam generation needs softened, treated water; produced water is recovered, cleaned and reused. Every barrel of steam is also a barrel of water to treat, and every barrel of water treated is a cost that does not appear in the oil rate.

The SAGD test

Does the reservoir have enough thickness and vertical permeability for a steam chamber to grow, and is the pay continuous enough that the chamber does not leak into a thief zone? If not, SAGD is the wrong tool. CSS and HASD are the Faja family.

04 Lift

A screw inside a rubber tube

A progressing-cavity pump is a positive-displacement pump with two parts: a helical steel rotor and an elastomer stator with one more lobe. The rotor turns inside the stator and forms sealed cavities that travel up the pump. Flow is proportional to speed. The pump tolerates viscous, sandy fluid, which is why it became the predominant artificial lift in several Faja fields (SPE 165061).

Its limits are mechanical and thermal. The elastomer has a temperature ceiling. The pump has a differential-pressure limit. Gas can break the seal between cavities, and sand abrades both rotor and stator. Run life is a materials and operating problem, not a theoretical one (SPE 191118). A PCP is not a universal answer, and not every Faja well uses one.

Lift Handles Weak point
PCP Viscous oil, sand, moderate gas; flow proportional to speed Elastomer temperature, differential pressure, gas, wear
ESP Higher volumes; multistage centrifugal lift Sand and very high viscosity; gas handling; cable and motor failures
Rod pump Lower volumes; simple surface drive Rod loading, deviation, abrasive wear

The comparison is qualitative; the brief’s sources do not support a single volume or depth ranking. Historical Cerro Negro vertical and deviated wells produced 200–600 bbl/d of 8° API oil at about 2,000 cP with rod pumps and top-drive PCPs; ESPs arrived for higher volumes (SPE 97889).

Gas is the other lift problem. A PCP can tolerate some free gas, but too much gas reduces volumetric efficiency; an ESP needs a gas separator or a recirculation path. The produced fluid is a mixture, and the pump has to be selected for the mixture, not for the oil rate on its own.

05 Separation and water

The surface train starts by splitting the stream

A well does not produce oil. It produces a mixture of oil, water, gas and sand. The three-phase separator is the first place the mixture becomes separate streams: gas off the top, oil from the oil layer, water from the bottom. The separator is a pressure vessel, not a tank, and its internals are chosen for the fluid: inlet momentum breakers, coalescing plates, weirs, mist eliminators.

Sand is removed upstream or from the separator bottoms. A sand knock-out and a water boot are the unglamorous parts that decide whether the plant stays online. Sand fills vessels, erodes valves and travels with the water. The gathering network has to be designed to keep it moving rather than let it settle in a low spot (SPE 184968).

Water cut rises over field life. Early on, the separator may be oil-rich; later, water dominates the liquid stream and the water-treatment capacity becomes the bottleneck. Produced water is not disposable by default: it is treated, recycled to steam generation where the chemistry allows, or disposed of under permit.

Gas has a use. Separator gas can be treated and sent to the steam generators as fuel, which closes a loop between the separator and the thermal system. The gas line in Fig. 1 runs from the separator scrubber back to the generator burner.

06 Blend

Diluent is a circulating cost

Extra-heavy crude cannot meet a pipeline viscosity specification on its own. The usual answer is diluent: naphtha or a light hydrocarbon, blended into the crude at 20–35 vol% to reach a pumpable blend. The blend’s API gravity and viscosity become the custody-transfer specifications, so the mixer is a commercial instrument as much as a piece of pipe.

Diluent is a loop, not a one-way additive. It has to be sourced, transported to the field, blended, transported to the upgrader and recovered there. Diluent availability and price are first-order operating constraints. A field that can produce oil but cannot source diluent is a field with a storage problem.

Cerro Negro also tested downhole diluent injection, adding light hydrocarbon before the pump to reduce viscosity in the wellbore (SPE 69433). The surface blend is still needed for the pipeline; downhole dilution changes where the viscosity problem is solved, not whether it is solved.

A static mixer is a length of pipe with fixed helical elements. It has no moving parts. The elements split, rotate and recombine the stream, so the blend that reaches the custody meter is not a two-layer slug of diluent and crude. The open mixer in Fig. 1 shows the elements; the real housing is a closed spool.

07 Upgrade

Reject carbon, then add hydrogen

An upgrader is not a refinery. It is a front-end facility that turns extra-heavy crude or blend into a lighter, sweeter synthetic crude that a conventional refinery can process. The common configuration is carbon rejection followed by hydrogen addition: a delayed coker and a hydrotreater.

In a delayed coker, vacuum residue is heated to about 480–505 °C in a furnace and soaked in a coke drum. The heavy molecules crack; coke deposits in the drum and lighter gas oil leaves the top. Coke yield is roughly 18–30 wt% of resid feed. The drums are quenched and decoked with high-pressure water, which is why they are tall vessels with repeat cycles rather than continuous reactors (delayed coker literature).

The coker’s gas oil still contains sulfur, nitrogen, metals and aromatics. The hydrotreater adds hydrogen over a catalyst to remove heteroatoms and saturate aromatics. Hydrogen is not free: it is produced or purchased, and it carries its own energy and carbon cost.

One Orinoco upgrader, designed for 180,000 b/d, took an 8° API feed with 4.3 wt% sulfur and 3.2 TAN, blended with naphtha, and produced a 32° API synthetic crude with 0.2 wt% sulfur (NACE 2006). That is one useful anchor, not a description of every upgrader. The exact product slate depends on the feed, the configuration and the hydrogen balance.

Upgrading does not make the byproducts disappear. Petroleum coke, sulfur and metals have to be sold, stockpiled or managed, and the process emits CO₂. A barrel of syncrude is cleaner at the refinery gate than a barrel of extra-heavy crude, but the carbon and the contaminants have moved, not vanished.

Export blends are designated grades. Venezuela’s Merey blend is a heavy sour grade of roughly 16° API; its exact gravity and sulfur move with the blend. The site says “roughly” for a reason.

09 Boundaries

What this model is not

It is not a reservoir simulator. It does not solve flow through rock, history-match a field, forecast a decline or price a barrel. It does not know the thickness of the pay at a particular location, the salinity of the produced water or the availability of diluent next quarter.

It is a deterministic systems model: one well, one steady operating point, four levers and a small set of outputs. Its job is to make the trade-offs visible. Raising steam can raise the oil rate and the SOR at the same time. Raising diluent lowers viscosity and raises API. A slow pump can cap the rate before the reservoir does. Those directions are the lesson; the magnitudes are illustrative.

The distinction matters because the oil industry is full of precise numbers attached to uncertain models. A generated number without a boundary is a false promise. Here the boundary is printed beside the controls.

10 Field notes

A short history of the Faja

Context, not politics. Dates are drawn from the sources below; current production is deliberately not quantified.

  1. 1936

    La Canoa-1

    The first well in the belt is drilled near La Canoa, Anzoátegui. It is a test, not a development.

  2. 1990s

    Strategic associations

    Four heavy-oil associations open the belt to integrated upstream and upgrading projects.

  3. 2007

    Majority control

    The government takes majority control of the associations, changing the operating and investment structure.

  4. 2010s

    Decline

    Production falls as investment, maintenance and diluent supply lag. The resource does not change.

  5. 2020s

    Capacity, not geology

    The constraint is surface capacity, capital and operations. The belt remains a very large in-place resource.

11 Glossary

The vocabulary

Terms are defined on first use in the prose; this is the place to come back to.

Extra-heavy crude
Liquid petroleum with API gravity below 10° and reservoir viscosity above 10,000 cP under the World Energy Council definition. The Faja is commonly described as 8–10° API with viscosities in the thousands of centipoise.
API gravity
An inverse density scale: higher API means lighter oil. Orinoco extra-heavy crude is roughly 8–10° API; a 32° API synthetic crude is much lighter.
Viscosity
A fluid’s resistance to flow, measured in centipoise (cP). It falls steeply as temperature rises, which is why heat and diluent are the two levers in this system.
Oficina Formation
The Miocene sandstone formation that holds most Orinoco Belt oil; fluvial, nearshore-marine and tidal sands, largely unconsolidated.
Net pay
The thickness of oil-saturated reservoir rock that can contribute flow. Faja net pay ranges from a few feet to hundreds of feet.
OOIP
Original oil in place: the oil estimated to be in the reservoir before production. It is not the same as reserves or recoverable oil.
Technically recoverable
The fraction of OOIP that existing or foreseeable technology could extract, regardless of price. It is not a production rate.
Recovery factor
The fraction of OOIP that a process can recover. Cold production in the Faja is commonly single digits to low teens; thermal methods can reach several times that.
Cold production
Producing heavy oil without adding heat, using horizontal wells, sand management and artificial lift. It is the baseline development method in the Faja.
Horizontal well
A well drilled laterally through the reservoir to expose hundreds to thousands of metres of pay. Faja developments use single- and multi-lateral horizontals from pads.
Multilateral well
A well with more than one lateral branch from a single main bore. It increases reservoir contact per surface location.
Sand control
The completion design and operating practice that manages unconsolidated sand: slotted liners, screens, gravel packs or controlled sand production.
Foamy oil
A cold-production flow regime in which dissolved gas comes out of solution as tiny bubbles, helping to drive viscous oil toward the well.
Artificial lift
Any mechanical method that lifts fluid when the reservoir lacks enough pressure to do it. PCPs and ESPs are the main options in the Faja.
PCP
Progressing-cavity pump. A helical steel rotor turns inside an elastomer stator, forming sealed cavities that move fluid upward. It tolerates viscous, sandy fluid but is limited by elastomer temperature, gas and wear.
ESP
Electrical submersible pump. A multistage centrifugal pump run on a downhole motor; it handles higher volumes but is less tolerant of sand and very high viscosity than a PCP.
Well pad
A clustered surface location from which many directional or horizontal wells are drilled. The Faja is developed as a field of pads, not a grid of single vertical wells.
Christmas tree
The assembly of valves, spools and fittings on a wellhead that controls flow.
CSS
Cyclic steam stimulation, or “huff and puff”: steam injection, soak, then production from the same well. The cycle repeats until the steam-oil ratio becomes uneconomic.
Steam flooding
Continuous injection of steam through injector wells to drive oil toward separate producer wells.
HASD
Horizontal alternating steam drive: a steam-flood configuration with horizontal wells, studied for the Faja’s thin, heterogeneous sands.
SAGD
Steam-assisted gravity drainage: a pair of stacked horizontal wells, with steam injected into the upper well and oil draining to the lower. It requires thick pay and is not the default Faja process.
Steam quality
The mass fraction of steam that is vapour in a steam–water mixture. Conventional generators produce roughly 70–80% quality; high-quality pilots reach 95%+.
SOR
Steam-oil ratio: barrels of steam (cold-water equivalent) consumed per barrel of oil produced. It is the thermal system’s key efficiency and emissions driver.
Diluent
A light hydrocarbon, usually naphtha or condensate, blended into extra-heavy crude to lower viscosity and meet pipeline specifications.
Blend
The mixture of extra-heavy crude and diluent that is transported by pipeline. Its API gravity and viscosity are the custody-transfer specifications.
Three-phase separator
A pressure vessel that separates produced fluid into gas, oil and water.
Water cut
The fraction of produced liquid that is water. It rises over field life and increases the load on separation and water treatment.
Upgrader
A facility that converts extra-heavy crude or blend into a lighter, sweeter synthetic crude, typically through carbon rejection and hydrotreating.
Delayed coker
A thermal cracking unit that heats resid to about 480–505 °C and deposits coke in drums, producing gas oil and lighter liquids.
Hydrotreater
A catalytic unit that adds hydrogen to remove sulfur, nitrogen and metals and saturate aromatics.
Syncrude
Synthetic crude oil produced by upgrading. One Orinoco example is a 32° API, 0.2 wt% sulfur product from an 8° API, 4.3 wt% sulfur feed.
Merey
Venezuela’s heavy sour export blend, roughly 16° API; its exact properties vary with the blend.
Carbon intensity
CO₂-equivalent emissions per barrel of oil, across a defined boundary. The site uses an illustrative well-to-tank boundary and says so.
Faja
Short for Faja Petrolífera del Orinoco, the Orinoco Oil Belt. Used as the project’s proper name for the subject.
Illustrative model
A deterministic, bounded model that demonstrates system behaviour and trade-offs. It is not a reservoir simulator and does not predict a specific field.
